# Jev Halves the Bill; Accuracy Stays a Coin Flip

Eight independent head-to-head datasets run between 6 October and 12 November 2026 put TypeSafe's Jev (jev-1.13/1.14, **$0.042 per million input tokens**) and OpenAI's Decisions API on gpt-6-luna (**$0.10 per million**, GA since 22 October) within noise of each other on accuracy, which makes price and operational shape the deciding factors: Jev costs **1.8–2.4x less per decision** at every payload size measured ($11–105 versus $24–224 per million decisions), matches Decisions on median latency (~140–300 ms for both) but has a far lighter tail (**p99 526 ms versus 1,469 ms** over 2,208 paired calls), and is better calibrated on 2–6-option questions, while Decisions is deterministic run to run, better calibrated on 77-way intent sets, and the only one of the two with production image input, JSON input, conversation and tool-call history, prompt caching, a ~1.05M-token window, GPT-Live voice integration, and enterprise controls (ZDR, HIPAA, US and EU regional processing, Private Link, Azure). The field has filled in around them in the past five weeks: Microsoft-Decision-1 matched Jev's price to the cent inside Azure (9 Oct), Google's Gemini Decision 1 matched OpenAI's price with audio, video and PDF input (11 Nov) and ties Jev at 66 on the only independent cross-vendor index, Cloudflare's Clef-flash and a dozen small vendors now undercut Jev at $0.004–$0.04, and the open-weight Laya is free but loses badly on anything beyond binary English checks. The practical rule that falls out of the evidence is simple: default to Jev for high-volume text decisions where cost and tail latency dominate and the data can leave for a US endpoint; pick OpenAI Decisions when the input is images, JSON, agent transcripts or voice, when a large shared prefix can be cached, when reruns must be reproducible, or when ZDR, HIPAA or EU processing is contractual; treat Gemini Decision 1 as the multimodal option to re-evaluate once it leaves v1beta; and run any of them only after tuning the confidence threshold on a few hundred of your own labelled items, because threshold choice moved both models' accuracy by 3–18 points in these tests, more than the gap between them.

## List prices differ 2.4x and measured bills differ 1.8–2.4x

TypeSafe's models page, fetched 14 November, still lists both `jev-1.13.0` and `jev-1.14.0` at **$0.042 per million input tokens with free output**, unchanged since the 15 September launch and through Jev 1.14 on 14 October; there is no batch rate, free tier, cache discount or published statement that the price is temporary ([docs.typesafe.ai/models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models); [mrjev.com changelog](https://mrjev.com/changelog/)). OpenAI's Decisions guide prices uncached input at **$0.10 per million**, charges nothing for output, discounts cache hits and charges cache writes, with "regional processing premiums and long-context input multipliers" applying; the cached rate is reported by three outlets as $0.01 per million, the long-context tier doubles input above 272K tokens, and regional (data-residency) endpoints carry a 10% uplift ([OpenAI Decisions guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/decisions); [OpenAI pricing docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing); [Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/openai-makes-decisions-api-generally-available-with-gpt-6-luna/)). Decisions has no Batch, Flex or Fast row on OpenAI's pricing page, so the 50% discount levers available on the Responses API do not appear to exist for it ([OpenAI pricing docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)).

The measured cost per decision tracks the list ratio closely because both models bill only input. Across the tests that recorded tokens, Jev came out at roughly **$30–40 per million decisions at 500–800-token payloads** and **$105 per million at ~2,500 tokens**, against **$40–60 and $224** for Decisions: Nexus's eight guardrail checks on realistic agent payloads cost $0.11 versus $0.22 per thousand (2.1x), Evaboot's lead scoring $0.034 versus $0.060 (1.76x), van Deth's Dutch texts $0.011–0.016 versus $0.024–0.031 per thousand, Arize's RAGTruth run $0.045 versus $0.090 per thousand, and Hightouch's identity matching $0.05 versus $0.10 per thousand identities ([agent.nexus](https://agent.nexus/blog/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev); [Evaboot](https://evaboot.com/blog/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev); [vincentvandeth.nl](https://vincentvandeth.nl/blog/jev-vs-openai-decisions-api-vs-haiku-5-5-benchmark); [Arize](https://arize.com/blog/decision-model-benchmark.md); [Hightouch](https://hightouch.com/blog/llm-judges-identity-resolution)). The ratio shrinks on very short payloads because Jev adds roughly **260 fixed tokens per call** while Decisions adds roughly 150 per question; jev-measured's 633 short items came out only 1.3x apart ($30 versus $40 per million), and Nexus's multi-question workload still favoured Jev 2.1x ([WallerChen/jev-measured](https://github.com/WallerChen/jev-measured); [agent.nexus](https://agent.nexus/blog/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev)). Both remain one to two orders of magnitude below frontier chat models used as judges: Opus 5.5 cost $3.86–5.39 per thousand identities against Jev's $0.05–0.06, and Arize priced Opus 5 at $14.30 per thousand RAGTruth rows ([Hightouch](https://hightouch.com/blog/llm-judges-identity-resolution); [Arize](https://arize.com/blog/decision-model-benchmark.md)).

Two structural levers can flip the cost ranking. OpenAI's prompt caching puts an implicit breakpoint at the end of the shared `input`, requires a reusable prefix of at least 1,024 tokens (question content does not count), supports only a 30-minute TTL and disallows breakpoints inside question instructions ([OpenAI Decisions guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/decisions)). A workload that re-evaluates many questions against one large, stable document can therefore run Decisions below Jev's price on cache hits, but per-record state defeats caching entirely: Parallel's worked example of 1,000 records times five questions on 1,500-token state came to about $0.076 on Jev and $0.18 on OpenAI, widening to $0.41 versus $0.98 with 8,000 tokens of web text per record, with caching contributing nothing ([Parallel](https://parallel.ai/articles/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev-vs-parallel.md)). The other lever, few-shot examples, does not change the winner: on BANKING77 two examples per intent (154 in the prompt) lifted Decisions from 78.96% to 84.16% and Jev from 81.17% to 85.32%, so the gap held, while Decisions' token bill rose 2.86x and its mean latency 35% ([stacktoheap](https://stacktoheap.com/blog/2026/10/08/jev-the-decisions-strike-back/)). Neither API exposes a dedicated examples field; OpenAI's reference lists no such parameter and Jev users place examples in the state ([OpenAI API reference](https://developers.openai.com/api/reference/resources/decisions/methods/create); [Towards AI](https://pub.towardsai.net/tuning-jev-part-3-tuning-is-task-specific-four-places-to-put-few-shot-examples-751ab60e90e9)).

## Eight datasets, no accuracy winner, and the big gaps come from vendors

Pooling the four largest datasets with the least commercial stake (Nexus 2,155 guardrail cases, BTInsights 2,617 survey responses, jev-measured 633 items, van Deth 600 answers) leaves the accuracy gap under one point in either direction. Nexus's eight yes/no guardrail checks produced **221 Jev errors (10.3%, 95% CI 9.0–11.6%) against 233 Decisions errors (10.8%, CI 9.6–12.2%)**, with the AUC difference crossing zero on seven of eight checks ([agent.nexus](https://agent.nexus/blog/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev)). BTInsights' open-end coding against two human coders scored **F1 72.2 versus 72.9 at default strictness**, "within margin of error," and 75.4 versus 77.1 after each model's threshold was tuned on a separate sample ([BTInsights](https://btinsights.ai/blog/jev-vs-openai-decisions-api-coding-accuracy/)). jev-measured's fixed-seed sample of AG News, MASSIVE, Banking77, WikiToxic, Amazon polarity and Emotion gave **94.0% versus 94.1% on simple choices, 79.0% versus 79.8% on 59–77-option tasks and 95.0% versus 93.5% on yes/no** ([WallerChen/jev-measured](https://github.com/WallerChen/jev-measured)). Van Deth's preregistered 450 Dutch business texts, the one test with a paired significance test, found Jev made 41 errors of 600, Decisions 30 and Claude Haiku 5.5 at low effort 15; the Decisions-versus-Jev difference was not significant (McNemar p=0.09) while Haiku beat both (p<0.001 and p=0.002) at roughly 3–6x the cost and 10x the latency ([vincentvandeth.nl](https://vincentvandeth.nl/blog/jev-vs-openai-decisions-api-vs-haiku-5-5-benchmark)).

The large gaps all come from vendor-adjacent sources and from task shapes where Decisions' answer distribution shifts rather than from raw discrimination. Evaboot's **71% versus 46% exact-level** result on four-level lead scoring coexists with rank correlations of 0.87 and 0.85 against the labels and 0.94 between the two models; Decisions was "systematically one level stricter," and in-sample threshold tuning narrowed the gap to 73% versus 64% ([Evaboot](https://evaboot.com/blog/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev)). Respan, which sells its own Span decision models, relays Kartik Anand's 500 synthetic routing cases where Decisions scored **macro-F1 71.4% against Jev's 89.2%** and picked the same route across six option orders only 64.2% of the time versus Jev's 91.6%; the primary post could not be located, so the figure should be held loosely until replicated ([Respan](https://www.respan.ai/articles/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev)). In the other direction, Hightouch's 97.3% versus 95.7% on easy identity pairs and BTInsights' 77.1 versus 75.4 after tuning are the two defended Decisions wins, both on binary, entity-matching or coding tasks with tuned cutoffs ([Hightouch](https://hightouch.com/blog/llm-judges-identity-resolution); [BTInsights](https://btinsights.ai/blog/jev-vs-openai-decisions-api-coding-accuracy/)). Arize's RAGTruth result (Jev 85.5% balanced accuracy and ROC-AUC 0.93 against 81.5% and 0.90) is the most relevant number for RAG verification work and favours Jev by four points, with both models stable across two question wordings while Liquid d1 collapsed from AUC 0.92 to 0.17 on the "unsupported claim" phrasing ([Arize](https://arize.com/blog/decision-model-benchmark.md)).

Two ceilings sit above both models. On narrow, well-labelled intent sets a trained classifier still wins: MiniLM reached **89.87% on BANKING77** against 85.32% for Jev and 84.16% for Decisions with examples ([stacktoheap](https://stacktoheap.com/blog/2026/10/08/jev-the-decisions-strike-back/)). On hard sets a frontier LLM wins by 3–6 points at 10–90x the cost: Opus 5.5 scored 99.0% and 95.1% on Hightouch's easy and hard identity sets against Jev's 95.7% and 89.1% ([Hightouch](https://hightouch.com/blog/llm-judges-identity-resolution)). And neither decision model knows anything outside its input: on Parallel's 300 dated web-fact questions without supplied context Decisions scored 41.7% and Jev 1.14 33.4% against a 42.4% random baseline ([Parallel](https://parallel.ai/articles/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev-vs-parallel.md)).

## Jev is sharper on small option sets, Decisions is stricter, steadier and deterministic

Calibration evidence splits along option count. On van Deth's 3–6-option Dutch tasks Jev's expected calibration error was **0.014 against 0.054 for Decisions**, and Jev's answers at 90% or higher confidence were right 98.7% of the time; Arize's 655 evaluator cases gave calibration error of **0.023 for Jev and 0.042 for Decisions** ([vincentvandeth.nl](https://vincentvandeth.nl/blog/jev-vs-openai-decisions-api-vs-haiku-5-5-benchmark); [Arize](https://arize.com/blog/decision-model-benchmark.md)). On the 77-way BANKING77 the order reverses: Decisions' 10-bin ECE was **4.35% against Jev's 9.33%** (4.63% versus 6.50% with examples), although Jev's Brier score was slightly better in both conditions (0.3078 versus 0.3163; 0.2206 versus 0.2472) ([stacktoheap](https://stacktoheap.com/blog/2026/10/08/jev-the-decisions-strike-back/)). jev-measured's "wrong at 90% or higher confidence" rate on choice tasks puts Jev at 11.5% and Decisions at 9.2%, both far behind Cloudflare Clef at 2.3%, Kev 4B at 2.6% and Liquid d1 at 3.2%, and far ahead of Laya at 26.8% ([WallerChen/jev-measured](https://github.com/WallerChen/jev-measured)). Respan's cited Brier scores of 0.117 for Jev against 0.296 for Decisions come from the unlocated Anand routing test and share its caveat ([Respan](https://www.respan.ai/articles/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev)). Artificial Analysis found decision models as a class overconfident on hard knowledge questions ("accuracy falls below stated probabilities at almost every level" on HLE-MultiChoice and AA-Omniscience) but well calibrated on tool-call and safety tasks, and from 8 November its headline score became 50 x (1 + Accuracy - Brier), so calibration now counts as much as accuracy on the main independent leaderboard ([Artificial Analysis v1](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/artificial-analysis-decision-index); [Artificial Analysis v2](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/decision-index-v2)).

The operational finding that every tester converged on is that Decisions' probabilities skew conservative, so its useful threshold sits higher. Hightouch settled on 60% for Jev and 70% for Decisions; BTInsights tuned Jev to 55–85% and Decisions to 70–90%; van Deth's yes/no "needs a human" question had AUC 0.996 on Decisions but only 84% accuracy at a 0.5 cutoff because 24 of 77 no-escalation emails scored at or above 0.5, and a post-hoc 0.95 cutoff gave 97% ([Hightouch](https://hightouch.com/blog/llm-judges-identity-resolution); [BTInsights](https://btinsights.ai/blog/jev-vs-openai-decisions-api-coding-accuracy/); [vincentvandeth.nl](https://vincentvandeth.nl/blog/jev-vs-openai-decisions-api-vs-haiku-5-5-benchmark)). Nexus tuned its thresholds on Jev and never retuned them for Decisions, which plausibly inflates the Decisions error count in the one large guardrail test ([agent.nexus](https://agent.nexus/blog/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev)). Tuning moved accuracy by 3–4 F1 points for both models at BTInsights, by 18 points for Decisions at Evaboot and by 13 points on van Deth's escalation question, so the threshold choice is worth more than the model choice on most tasks.

Determinism is the one dimension where the two differ categorically. Decisions returned **identical scores on all 998 leads across three runs** and identical probabilities on 498 of 500 routing reruns; Jev changed about 10% of leads by one level (mean spread 0.03) and gave the same route on 95.2% of reruns, though it did return the same answer on 594 of 600 category questions 13 days apart ([Evaboot](https://evaboot.com/blog/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev); [Respan](https://www.respan.ai/articles/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev); [vincentvandeth.nl](https://vincentvandeth.nl/blog/jev-vs-openai-decisions-api-vs-haiku-5-5-benchmark)). Decisions' near-perfect repeatability points to near-greedy decoding and matters for audit trails and threshold rules that sit near the margin; Jev's small flip rate is a reason to pin `jev-1.14.0` rather than `jev-latest` and to keep a "review" band around the cutoff.

## Medians tie, Decisions' tail is 2.8x heavier, and gateways distort both

The only test that measured both tails over thousands of paired calls found medians essentially identical and tails sharply different: Nexus's 2,208 timed calls per model gave **269 ms versus 268 ms median, 388 versus 584 ms p95, 526 versus 1,469 ms p99**, with 0.14% of Jev calls and 2.15% of Decisions calls exceeding one second and 17 Decisions calls (none for Jev) exceeding two ([agent.nexus](https://agent.nexus/blog/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev)). Evaboot's 5,988 calls at six-way concurrency agreed on the median (0.26 s Jev, 0.27 s Decisions) with the slowest 5% above 0.31 and 0.33 s respectively ([Evaboot](https://evaboot.com/blog/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev)). Arize measured Jev at **80 ms p50 against 160 ms** for Decisions on RAGTruth, Hightouch 0.15 s against 0.17–0.20 s per identity, and QueryPanel saw an 875 ms cold first call on Decisions that Jev did not show ([Arize](https://arize.com/blog/decision-model-benchmark.md); [Hightouch](https://hightouch.com/blog/llm-judges-identity-resolution); [QueryPanel](https://querypanel.io/blog/jev-vs-openai-decisions-api-nl-to-sql)). Both are an order of magnitude faster than chat models used as judges: Haiku 5.5 took 3,105 ms per text at low effort in van Deth's run, Opus 5.5 2.2–2.6 s per identity at Hightouch, and Artificial Analysis clocked Jev 1.14 at 92 ms per task against 11.34 s for GPT-6 Luna at maximum reasoning through the chat API ([vincentvandeth.nl](https://vincentvandeth.nl/blog/jev-vs-openai-decisions-api-vs-haiku-5-5-benchmark); [Hightouch](https://hightouch.com/blog/llm-judges-identity-resolution); [Artificial Analysis v2](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/decision-index-v2)).

Tests that found Decisions faster all reached Jev through OpenRouter, and the gateway penalty is large enough to explain the disagreement. jev-measured's twelve interleaved runs put direct api.typesafe.ai at **313 ms p50 and 423 ms p90 against 734 ms and 1,739 ms via OpenRouter**, with server-side processing of only 70–81 ms; from a Vercel iad1 function Decisions measured 138 ms p50 against 184 ms for OpenRouter-routed Jev, and van Deth (all calls via OpenRouter) saw 179 ms for Decisions against 293 ms for Jev ([WallerChen/jev-measured](https://github.com/WallerChen/jev-measured); [vincentvandeth.nl](https://vincentvandeth.nl/blog/jev-vs-openai-decisions-api-vs-haiku-5-5-benchmark)). OpenAI's own "about 10x faster than the Responses API" claim, illustrated by a DevDay slide of roughly 150 ms against 1.6 s, is relative to its own chat endpoint and unconditioned; one tester calling Decisions on Azure East US measured 1.4–2.0 s for text requests and 2.4–5.5 s with an image, an order of magnitude above the slide ([OpenAI Decisions guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/decisions); [Firecrawl](https://www.firecrawl.dev/blog/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev); [Classmethod](https://dev.classmethod.jp/en/articles/azure-openai-decisions-api-ga/)). Decisions' heavy tail and cold start are the clearest liabilities for voice and real-time agent loops, where p99 sets the user-visible pause.

Jev's latency liability is geography and capacity. TypeSafe's status page lists only US endpoints (api.us-east-1, us-east-2, us-west-2) and no EU region exists, so every non-US caller pays a transatlantic round trip, while OpenAI offers US and EU regional processing for Decisions at a 10% uplift ([status.typesafe.ai](https://status.typesafe.ai); [OpenAI Decisions guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/decisions); [OpenAI pricing docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)). No published test measured either model's p95 from an EU or APAC vantage point. TypeSafe also cut rate limits on 30 September from 250,000 to 100,000 tokens per second and from 1,200 requests per minute to 40 per second, then stopped publishing limits on 14 October (they are now per-organization in the console, with increases via a support form), and its status page records a 45-minute "Elevated Latency" incident on 14 October and a 75-minute "System overload" on 30 October with uptime figures of 99.985%, 99.848% and 99.882% over unlabelled periods ([mrjev.com changelog](https://mrjev.com/changelog/); [docs.typesafe.ai/models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models); [status.typesafe.ai](https://status.typesafe.ai)). OpenAI publishes no Decisions-specific rate limits at all ([OpenAI API reference](https://developers.openai.com/api/reference/resources/decisions/methods/create)). Either way, a production integration needs a short timeout and a deterministic fallback.

## OpenAI sells inputs, caching and compliance; TypeSafe sells price, versioning and a status page

The two request contracts are near-isomorphic, which keeps switching costs low. OpenAI's `POST /v1/decisions` takes `model`, `input` and 1–400 `questions` of type `predicate` (probability 0–1), `choice` (2–255 options, returns chosen value, per-option probabilities and confidence) or `score` (2–10 ordered levels, returns a probability-weighted score plus distribution), mapping one-to-one onto Jev's Noul, Choice and Score; a refusal on one question leaves the others answered, and dependent questions need a second request ([OpenAI Decisions guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/decisions); [OpenAI API reference](https://developers.openai.com/api/reference/resources/decisions/methods/create)). Vercel, Portkey, LLM Gateway, Langfuse and Eden AI all expose OpenAI's `decisions.create` shape and route Jev through it, and Vercel's gateway supports decision fallbacks between the two models in one call ([Vercel AI Gateway docs](https://vercel.com/docs/ai-gateway/sdks-and-apis/openai-decisions); [Langfuse changelog](https://langfuse.com/changelog/2026-11-11-ai-gateway-openai-decisions.md); [Portkey](https://portkey.ai/docs/api-reference/inference-api/decisions.md)).

What OpenAI has and Jev lacks is mostly about what can go into the request. Decisions accepts images as base64 or public URLs (up to 128 per request per third-party tests), including inside individual question instructions since GA; `input_json` structured data; full conversation history with `function_call`, `function_call_output` and custom tool items; and an effective ~1.05M-token window versus Jev's 64K per request and 32K per question ([OpenAI Decisions guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/decisions); [OpenAI API reference](https://developers.openai.com/api/reference/resources/decisions/methods/create); [OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/openai/gpt-6-luna-decisions); [Parallel](https://parallel.ai/articles/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev-vs-parallel.md)). Since 15 October GPT-Live can "call Decisions directly through native delegation" or fire decisions as transcript deltas arrive, and OpenAI's GA materials relay Speak cutting time-to-next-action from 1.4 s to 0.13 s and Sierra reaching under 280 ms p95 for next-action selection at 90% lower cost per call ([OpenAI API changelog](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/changelog); [Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/openai-makes-decisions-api-generally-available-with-gpt-6-luna/)). Jev's image support exists only behind a `TypeSafe-Danger-Zone-Images` header (PNG/JPEG stills, performance that "still trails" text), its 262K long-context mode is likewise opt-in and "slower and less accurate," and TypeSafe says it cannot vouch for either in production ([systemonemodels.org](https://systemonemodels.org/guides/jev-1-14/); [docs.typesafe.ai/models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models); [WinBuzzer](https://winbuzzer.com/2026/10/24/openai-ai-decision-service-leaves-beta-with-voice-integration-xcxwbn/)). Jev has no caching, batch API, fine-tuning or few-shot field ([docs.typesafe.ai/models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models)).

What Jev has and OpenAI lacks is control and breadth of distribution. TypeSafe exposes pinnable `jev-1.13.0` and `jev-1.14.0` with `jev-latest` as a moving alias and informal promises of 60 and 30 days' deprecation notice; OpenAI accepts only `gpt-6-luna` with no snapshot, so decision behaviour can change under a customer without warning ([docs.typesafe.ai/models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models); [systemonemodels.org](https://systemonemodels.org/guides/jev-1-14/); [OpenAI API reference](https://developers.openai.com/api/reference/resources/decisions/methods/create)). Jev 1.14 adds a `box` question type returning a bounding box with confidence, which OpenAI has no equivalent for ([MarkTechPost](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/10/14/typesafe-ai-jev-1-14-images-box-long-context/)). Jev is served first-party and via OpenRouter, Cloudflare Workers AI, Vercel, Eden AI and Venice; TypeSafe states Jev "is not trained on customer requests or responses," offers ZDR to enterprise customers on request, publishes a DPA with EU standard contractual clauses and a 72-hour incident-notice commitment, and runs a public status page, but publishes no SLA, no retention period and no EU, UK or Canada residency ([Firecrawl](https://www.firecrawl.dev/blog/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev); [docs.typesafe.ai/models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models); [colchix](https://colchix.com/blog/can-european-enterprises-use-jev); [status.typesafe.ai](https://status.typesafe.ai)). OpenAI's Decisions guide states ZDR and HIPAA are "supported for eligible customers," with data residency in all supported regions, regional processing in the US and Europe (EEA plus Switzerland) and Private Link; the data-controls page marks `/v1/decisions` as ZDR-eligible "with limitations" requiring sales approval, and Decisions reached Azure OpenAI and Microsoft Foundry on 22 October ([OpenAI Decisions guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/decisions); [beri.net](https://www.beri.net/article/openai-decisions-api-public-beta-gpt-6-luna-pricing-cache-discount-vs-jev-clef-zdr-residency); [Classmethod](https://dev.classmethod.jp/en/articles/azure-openai-decisions-api-ga/)). For a regulated buyer this is the single largest gap between the products, and it points the other way from price.

TypeSafe's corporate position reduces the risk that Jev's price is a loss leader about to be withdrawn. The company closed an **$870M Series A at a $7.5B post-money valuation on 9 October** led by Andreessen Horowitz with Sequoia and DCVC, its CEO told Bloomberg it is profitable after expenses, and on a 15 October podcast he said Jev was processing about one trillion tokens a day three weeks after launch with roughly a third of the Fortune 500 using it ([Channel Insider](https://www.channelinsider.com/ai/news-typesafe-ai-870-million-funding-jev-model/); [Scuttlebutt](https://daily.scuttlebutt.co/p/jev-hit-1t-tokens-a-day-3-weeks-after)). The price has survived two hyperscaler launches without moving, and the only hint of subsidy is a secondary review's unsourced paraphrase ([Flowtivity](https://flowtivity.ai/blog/jev-typesafe-ai-decision-model/)). The rate-limit cuts and the 30 October overload incident read as capacity strain under demand rather than retreat.

## Gemini ties Jev, Microsoft matches its price, and small vendors now undercut everyone

Google's Gemini Decision 1 (Wednesday 11 November) is the most consequential entrant because it copies OpenAI's price and exceeds everyone on input breadth: **$0.10 per million input tokens**, output free, shared input billed once per request regardless of question count, 1–1,000 questions per request, a 262,144-token context, and text, images, audio, video and PDF mixed in one request, with a free AI Studio tier of 200 requests and 50,000 tokens per minute ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/googles-gemini-decision-1-model-scores-up-to-1-000-questions-in-one-api-call); [eesel.ai Gemini](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/gemini-decisions-api)). Google's vendor-run suite claims **92.2% on JevBench against 90.9% for GPT-6 Luna and 90.0% for Jev 1.14**, calibration error of 0.0250 against Jev's 0.0494 and "nearly 5x cheaper" than Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, while conceding Jev leads on Mind2Web Multimodal (86.8% versus 86.2%) and When2Call (82.0% versus 79.9%); none of these figures has been independently reproduced ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/googles-gemini-decision-1-model-scores-up-to-1-000-questions-in-one-api-call); [eesel.ai Gemini](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/gemini-decisions-api)). The one independent reading, Artificial Analysis Decision Index v2.1 on 11 November, scores Gemini Decision 1 at **66, tied with Jev 1.14 at 9th of 18**, behind Nace's Drex Max v1.1 and Fastino's GLiDE v1.2 at about 72.6, with Gemini among the pricier models on the index at $345 per million tasks and Jev ahead on When2Call (80.8% versus 75.2%) and on the long-document AA-Paperclip set (35.5% versus 33%) ([Artificial Analysis on X](https://x.com/ArtificialAnlys/status/2120582049522127205); [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/googles-gemini-decision-1-model-scores-up-to-1-000-questions-in-one-api-call)). It is still v1beta on the Gemini Developer API, not on Vertex AI, with no stated SLA or region, and one Hacker News tester reported a prompt blocked under Google's prohibited-use policy for "sensitive words" ([eesel.ai Gemini](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/gemini-decisions-api); [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50456791)).

Microsoft-Decision-1 (9 October) is a post-trained Qwen3.5-9B that returns probabilities over fixed options, text-only, 32,768-token context, priced at **$0.042 per million**, exactly Jev's rate, on Microsoft Foundry (GA v1, Azure billing, Azure SLAs) and OpenRouter ([eesel.ai Microsoft](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/microsoft-decision-1-pricing); [MarkTechPost](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/10/09/microsoft-ai-releases-microsoft-decision-1-a-qwen3-5-9b-decision-scoring-model/)). Microsoft's vendor-run 36-benchmark suite claims 83.5% average accuracy against 82.3% for Jev 1.13 and 79.4% for GPT-6 Luna Decisions, with calibration of 92.2 behind Jev's 93.7 and a same-region median latency of 85 ms against 240 ms for Jev; independent boards are cooler, with Artificial Analysis v1 scoring it 54 against Jev's 58, v2.1 about 62 against 66, JevBench measuring 0.46 s median against the 85 ms claim, and AIM ranking it 10th at 58 with the best bias score of the top ten (90.0) but the weakest browser-task score (46 of 100) ([eesel.ai Microsoft](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/microsoft-decision-1-pricing); [Artificial Analysis v1](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/artificial-analysis-decision-index); [OrcaRouter](https://www.orcarouter.ai/blog/gemini-decision-1-vs-microsoft-decision-1); [pinggy.io](https://pinggy.io/blog/best_ai_decision_models/); [AIM](https://aimultiple.com/system-one)). Microsoft compared cost only against GPT-6 Sol, not Jev, which reads as an admission that it is not claiming a price advantage; its value is Azure procurement, not a better model.

Laya, the open-weight Jev-compatible model from Convai, is the sovereignty option and the accuracy laggard. It is Apache 2.0, runs a ModernBERT-large encoder with a 512-token context (or a multilingual mmBERT variant at 1,024 tokens), serves a `/v1/systemone` endpoint, and is free to self-host, but jev-measured's paired runs show it collapsing on anything multi-way: **Banking77 44% against Jev's 82%, MASSIVE 45% against 76%, Amazon polarity 67% against 98%**, with 26.8% of its answers wrong at 90% or higher confidence against Jev's 11.5%, and Respan's adversarial test found a paired flip rate of 0.298 and injection success of 0.396 against Jev's 0.021 and 0.063 ([awesome-system-one](https://github.com/andyrewlee/awesome-system-one); [WallerChen/jev-measured](https://github.com/WallerChen/jev-measured); [Respan](https://www.respan.ai/articles/jev-alternatives)). It scored 26.1% on AIM's bias test and is unranked there ([AIM](https://aimultiple.com/system-one)). Laya's one disputed win, 0.766 against Jev's 0.727 on 2,000 decisions, came from Convai's own test in which Laya never actually ran Jev ([knolli.ai](https://knolli.ai/post/jev-vs-laya)).

The more serious pressure on Jev comes from hosted specialists below its price. Cloudflare cut Clef-flash from $0.09 to **$0.038** on 9 October, the day Microsoft launched; Nace's Drex Max v1.1 is reported at $0.03 and tops Artificial Analysis v2.1 with a 63% all-answers-correct rate on the 1,500-document AA-Paperclip set against Jev's 35.5%; Bespoke Nimble sells at $0.04, Cicero at $0.01 and Celeris-1 at $0.004 after a 5 November cut; Liquid d1 beat both Jev and Decisions on jev-measured's many-option tasks (86.0% against 79.0% and 79.8%) at $0.0078 per thousand ([eesel.ai Clef](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/cloudflare-clef-pricing); [awesome-system-one](https://github.com/andyrewlee/awesome-system-one); [Artificial Analysis v2](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/decision-index-v2); [WallerChen/jev-measured](https://github.com/WallerChen/jev-measured)). The leaderboards disagree on Jev's standing because they weight different things: it topped Artificial Analysis v1 among decision models at 58 on short knowledge, tool-call and safety questions, but the 8 November v2 re-weighting toward private long-document data pushed Drex Max and GLiDE to 73 and Jev to about 66, AIM ranks it 7th at 59 on browser-agent and bias tasks, and JevBench's capability view puts Jev 1.13 at 77.1 against 73.5 for OpenAI Decisions, the only like-for-like index gap found and available only second-hand ([Artificial Analysis v1](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/artificial-analysis-decision-index); [Artificial Analysis v2](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/decision-index-v2); [AIM](https://aimultiple.com/system-one); [pinggy.io](https://pinggy.io/blog/best_ai_decision_models/)). No independent leaderboard publishes an OpenAI Decisions row under the same methodology as Jev; Artificial Analysis lists "GPT-6 Luna (Decisions)" only in a calibration legend and AIM only in an FAQ ([Artificial Analysis v1](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/artificial-analysis-decision-index); [AIM](https://aimultiple.com/system-one)).

## Pick Jev for cheap text volume, Decisions for rich inputs and compliance

Because accuracy is tied, the choice reduces to input type, deployment constraints and cost structure, and the evidence supports a fairly crisp assignment of jobs.

| Job | Pick | Why, per the evidence |
|---|---|---|
| High-volume English text routing, moderation, yes/no guardrails, RAG relevance and hallucination gating, US-hosted | **Jev** (pin `jev-1.14.0`, call api.typesafe.ai directly) | Tied or ahead on accuracy (RAGTruth 85.5 vs 81.5; guardrails 10.3 vs 10.8% error), ~2x cheaper per decision, p99 2.8x lighter ([Arize](https://arize.com/blog/decision-model-benchmark.md); [agent.nexus](https://agent.nexus/blog/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev)) |
| Same workload inside an Azure estate | **Microsoft-Decision-1** or Jev | Same $0.042 price with Azure SLAs and billing; independent scores a few points below Jev ([eesel.ai Microsoft](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/microsoft-decision-1-pricing)) |
| Decisions over images (damage photos, document scans, UI screenshots) in production | **OpenAI Decisions** | Only production-rated image input of the two; Jev's images are opt-in and "trail" text ([OpenAI Decisions guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/decisions); [systemonemodels.org](https://systemonemodels.org/guides/jev-1-14/)) |
| Audio, video or PDF decisions | **Gemini Decision 1**, once out of v1beta | Only API accepting those inputs; ties Jev on AA v2.1 ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/googles-gemini-decision-1-model-scores-up-to-1-000-questions-in-one-api-call)) |
| Agent next-action selection over full conversation and tool-call history; voice agents on GPT-Live | **OpenAI Decisions** (budget for the tail) | Native message, tool-call and JSON inputs and GPT-Live delegation; watch p99 of 1.5 s and 875 ms cold starts ([OpenAI API changelog](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/changelog); [QueryPanel](https://querypanel.io/blog/jev-vs-openai-decisions-api-nl-to-sql)) |
| Many questions over one large, stable document (contracts, policies, long transcripts) | **OpenAI Decisions** with caching | 1.05M window and $0.01 cached input beat Jev's 64K/32K limits; Jev's 262K mode is opt-in and less accurate ([OpenAI Decisions guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/decisions); [docs.typesafe.ai/models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models)) |
| Regulated data: HIPAA, ZDR as a contract term, EU processing | **OpenAI Decisions** (hosted) or **self-hosted Laya/Clef/Strands Decider** | OpenAI alone offers ZDR, HIPAA, EU regional processing and Private Link; Jev is US-only with ZDR on request; self-hosting is the only full-sovereignty route ([OpenAI Decisions guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/decisions); [colchix](https://colchix.com/blog/can-european-enterprises-use-jev)) |
| Reproducible scoring for audits or A/B gates | **OpenAI Decisions** | Identical outputs across reruns on 998 leads; Jev flips ~5–10% at the margin ([Evaboot](https://evaboot.com/blog/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev)) |
| Ordinal scoring (lead fit, severity levels) | **Jev**, or Decisions with a re-centred rubric | Decisions scored one level stricter than labels (46% vs 71% exact) ([Evaboot](https://evaboot.com/blog/openai-decisions-api-vs-jev)) |
| 50+-way intent sets with labelled data available | **Train a small classifier**; otherwise Jev | MiniLM 89.87% beat both (85.32 / 84.16 with examples); Jev slightly more accurate, Decisions better calibrated at this width ([stacktoheap](https://stacktoheap.com/blog/2026/10/08/jev-the-decisions-strike-back/)) |
| Hard judgments where 3–6 points of accuracy are worth 10–90x cost | **Frontier LLM** (Opus/Haiku 5.5) with a decision model as the cheap first pass | Opus 5.5 99.0% vs Jev 95.7% on identity matching; Haiku beat both significantly on Dutch texts ([Hightouch](https://hightouch.com/blog/llm-judges-identity-resolution); [vincentvandeth.nl](https://vincentvandeth.nl/blog/jev-vs-openai-decisions-api-vs-haiku-5-5-benchmark)) |

Three rules apply whichever model wins the row. First, tune the threshold on your own labels and expect different operating points: Jev's useful cutoffs landed at 55–70% and Decisions' at 70–95% across four testers, and the swing from tuning (3–18 points) exceeded the gap between models everywhere ([BTInsights](https://btinsights.ai/blog/jev-vs-openai-decisions-api-coding-accuracy/); [Hightouch](https://hightouch.com/blog/llm-judges-identity-resolution); [vincentvandeth.nl](https://vincentvandeth.nl/blog/jev-vs-openai-decisions-api-vs-haiku-5-5-benchmark)). Second, call Jev directly rather than through OpenRouter when latency matters, since the gateway more than doubled p50 and quadrupled p90 in interleaved runs ([WallerChen/jev-measured](https://github.com/WallerChen/jev-measured)). Third, abstract the call behind the OpenAI `decisions.create` shape, which Vercel, Portkey, Langfuse and others already serve for both models, so that the provider can be swapped or used as a fallback without touching application logic ([Vercel AI Gateway docs](https://vercel.com/docs/ai-gateway/sdks-and-apis/openai-decisions)).

For the EddiGits transcript-search pipeline specifically, nothing in the new evidence changes the earlier recommendation to put one batched Jev call between ChromaDB and the LLM for per-chunk relevance, answerability and an "any answer exists" probability. That is a text-only, binary, US-hosted, cost-sensitive RAG-verification job, which is the row where Jev is both cheaper and measurably ahead (RAGTruth 85.5% versus 81.5%, 80 ms versus 160 ms p50) ([Arize](https://arize.com/blog/decision-model-benchmark.md)). Decisions would become the better fit only if the project started indexing video frames alongside Whisper transcripts, or if its users were in the EU and residency mattered.

## Conclusion

The most useful thing the past six weeks established is that the "decision model" category has converged on a wire format and a quality plateau faster than on a price: three hyperscalers and a dozen startups now return the same typed probabilities over the same three question shapes, independent tests cannot separate the two leaders on accuracy, and the real differentiation has moved to inputs (images, audio, JSON, agent transcripts), tails (p99, cold starts), compliance (ZDR, residency) and cost structure (per-call overhead versus cached prefixes). Jev's launch thesis that calibrated judgments can be sold like database lookups has been validated by its copiers, but the copiers also show the moat is operational rather than algorithmic; TypeSafe's $7.5B valuation is a bet that being first, cheapest and most stable in a commodity market is enough.

What to watch is not the next vendor benchmark but three things that would change the table above: whether Gemini Decision 1 reaches Vertex with an SLA and keeps its calibration lead once independent harnesses run it, whether TypeSafe ships an EU region or an SLA before enterprise buyers default to OpenAI or Azure for compliance reasons, and whether the sub-$0.04 specialists (Drex Max, Clef-flash, Liquid d1) hold their Artificial Analysis leads on long-document and many-option tasks, where Jev's 32K-per-question limit and 11.5% high-confidence error rate are its weakest documented points. Until then, the right engineering posture is the one every independent tester arrived at: run both on your own labelled set, tune each threshold separately, and let the cheaper one win the ties.
