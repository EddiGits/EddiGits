# Jev Earns Its Keep Gating Decisions, Not Generating Answers

Two weeks of independent testing since TypeSafe AI's 2026-09-15 launch point to one clear conclusion: Jev is a cheap, fast, reasonably calibrated zero-shot classifier whose best-evidenced uses are **few-class English routing and moderation, confidence-gated escalation in front of an LLM, and first-pass relevance filtering in RAG**, while its weakest are subjective scoring, rule-heavy domains (phishing 62.6%, invoices 61.8%) and anything requiring generation or arithmetic. Independent benchmarks put it level with mid-tier and 9B–70B open LLMs on Banking77-style intent tasks (**~81–83%**), a median **11.6 macro-F1 points** behind the best frontier model on a 7,977-item annotation suite, at **3–10x lower latency** and anywhere from **1.6x to ~580x lower cost** depending on the comparator; the vendor's 193.6x/444.6x headline is a best case against slow multi-step frontier workflows. For EddiGits, the fit is precise but narrow: Jev should not touch Whisper, embeddings or answer generation, but it maps directly onto three decision points the current `QueryEngine` handles crudely, namely replacing the cosine-only `MIN_SIMILARITY` gate with per-chunk relevance and answerability probabilities, deciding whether to spend an LLM call at all, and pre-tagging 30-second chunks at index time so queries like "where was I appreciated?" become metadata filters rather than embedding lookups. Each of these is a single batched call costing roughly a thousandth of a cent, callable today from Python through OpenRouter (direct signups paused 2026-09-22), and each needs a deterministic fallback because 90-day availability sits at 99.83% and thresholds must be validated on a few dozen hand-labelled transcript chunks before being trusted.

## A typed-decision model priced like a lookup, not a chat call

Jev is not a chat model. A single `POST https://api.typesafe.ai/v1/systemone` call takes a `state` (string or structured data) plus a dictionary of typed questions and returns typed answers with probability distributions; the three primitives are **Choice** (one of up to 255 unordered options, with per-option probabilities and a confidence scalar), **Score** (an ordered rubric of 2–10 levels) and **Noul** (a yes/no proposition returned as a single 0–1 probability with no separate confidence field) ([TypeSafe API reference](https://docs.typesafe.ai/api.md); [Primitives](https://docs.typesafe.ai/primitives.md)). All questions in a request are evaluated in parallel against the same state, so adding questions "barely changes response time" and costs only their extra tokens ([TypeSafe Introduction](https://docs.typesafe.ai/introduction)). Output tokens are always zero, and the price is **$0.042 per million input tokens** with output free, mirrored identically on Vercel AI Gateway, Cloudflare Workers AI and OpenRouter ([TypeSafe Models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models.md); [Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/ai/models/typesafe/jev/)). The hard limits that matter for a transcript application are a **32k-token budget for state plus the longest question** inside a 64k request cap, text-only input, and English-first accuracy with other languages "supported but less accurate" ([TypeSafe Models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models.md)).

The vendor's framing deserves translation. "Can't hallucinate" is a type-safety guarantee (a "0% type error rate ... mathematically guaranteed by design"), not an accuracy guarantee; TypeSafe itself publishes a "Model Jaggedness" page for Jev 1.13 listing eleven failure modes including literal reading of negations, unreliable arithmetic and counting, dates read as text rather than ordered quantities, accuracy that "falls as the state grows with content unrelated to the decision," no resistance to injected instructions, and no guarantee that P(yes) from a Noul matches the equivalent Choice (one worked example scored 0.22 as a Noul and 0.01/0.99 as a Choice) ([TypeSafe blog](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev); [Model Jaggedness](https://docs.typesafe.ai/model-jaggedness/jev-1.13.md)). The headline "193.6x faster and 444.6x cheaper" comes from TypeSafe's own workflow evaluations in which the reference labels are "the average of GPT-6 Astra and Fable 5.1" rather than human ground truth, and TypeSafe's own footnote says the figures "are on the higher end of real world gains" ([TypeSafe blog](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev); [dev.to, Ariful Islam](https://dev.to/arifulislamat/typesafes-jev-model-is-it-really-193x-faster-and-444x-cheaper-56oa)). On that same vendor benchmark, Jev scored **67.8% versus 74.1% for GPT-5.6 Sol**, and the widest per-workflow gap was invoice processing at **61.8% versus 79.1%** ([GreenNode](https://greennode.ai/blog/what-is-jev); [OrcaRouter](https://www.orcarouter.ai/blog/jev-vs-gpt-6-astra)).

Independent measurements consistently confirm the direction of the speed and cost claims while shrinking their magnitude. A 100-ticket, four-model triage test found Jev at a **474 ms median versus 1.9–3.5 s** for Sonnet 5, GPT-5.6 Sol and Gemini 3.8 Flash, and **31–65x cheaper** ([dev.to, Ariful Islam](https://dev.to/arifulislamat/typesafes-jev-model-is-it-really-193x-faster-and-444x-cheaper-56oa)). Against a cheap chat model the gap collapses: on 27 labelled triage examples Jev and mistral-small both scored 27/27 and the chat model cost only "1.4–1.7x" more ([WallerChen/jev-measured](https://github.com/WallerChen/jev-measured)), and a DIY logprob readout from Qwen 3.7 Flash was actually cheaper per decision ($0.006–0.010 versus $0.011–0.016 per thousand) while matching Jev's macro-F1 on Dutch text ([vincentvandeth.nl](https://vincentvandeth.nl/blog/jev-vs-diy-logprobs-typed-decisions-benchmark)). The honest summary from the most thorough roundup of eight days of tests is "level with mid-price LLMs and behind the frontier, at a fraction of the frontier's price" ([dev.to roundup](https://dev.to/gde/jev-after-eight-days-of-independent-tests-level-with-mid-price-llms-behind-the-frontier-1kln)).

Three cross-cutting findings explain the ranking. First, **decomposition is the strongest lever available**: the same phishing task scored 62.6% asked as one question and 95% when "split five ways" into atomic questions, which matches TypeSafe's own instruction to decompose any judgment that "weighs multiple independent factors" ([beri.net](https://www.beri.net/article/typesafe-jev-typed-decision-model-calibration-decomposition-shadow-eval); [TypeSafe docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/)). Second, latency is essentially **flat across option counts**, with p50 of 0.17–0.20 s from 2 to 151 options while LLMs degrade, so Jev's relative advantage grows with cardinality ([4esv/jev-eval](https://github.com/4esv/jev-eval)). Third, when labelled data exists in quantity, a small fine-tuned model beats Jev decisively: a 22M-parameter encoder plus logistic regression hit 93.2% on Banking77 against Jev's 80.1%, and a 4B LoRA trained on 1,000 phishing emails in 18 minutes reached 97.4% ([MindStudio benchmark](https://www.mindstudio.ai/blog/jev-vs-classic-classifiers-benchmark); [jev-phishing-bench issue #1](https://github.com/anisselbd/jev-phishing-bench/issues/1)). Jev's niche is therefore zero-label, many-domain, high-volume English classification, exactly the situation of a hobbyist or small-team application that cannot afford a labelling pass per feature.

## Calibration works for thresholds but breaks on option names and missing "none"

The single feature that distinguishes Jev from constrained decoding on any chat model is that its probabilities are trained to be calibrated via what TypeSafe calls Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions, and this claim has now been tested from several directions with mixed but mostly favourable results ([TypeSafe blog](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev)). On a 13-subset human-labelled suite Jev's **median ECE was 0.071**, lower than plain Gemma 4 on all 13 subsets ([dev.to roundup](https://dev.to/gde/jev-after-eight-days-of-independent-tests-level-with-mid-price-llms-behind-the-frontier-1kln)); on ticket triage it was 0.06–0.14 against 0.11–0.50 for open LLMs ([beese54](https://github.com/beese54/jev-ticket-triage)); on tool-call risk every incorrect answer came with confidence below 1.000 ([dev.to, webofmike](https://dev.to/webofmike/i-benchmarked-jev-on-agent-tool-call-risk-calibration-held-49i3)). The practical consequence is that a coverage/accuracy trade-off can be dialled in on day one without labels: in one 60-answer harness, accepting only confidence ≥0.7 gave 60% coverage at 100% accuracy, and ≥0.5 gave 75% coverage at 93.3% ([jev-kit](https://github.com/FlorianRiquelme/jev-kit)).

The counter-evidence is real enough to require validation rather than trust. The 7,977-item social-science suite found three frontier models better calibrated (median error 0.066 versus Jev's 0.157) and one task where Jev showed "high confidence while performing near chance" ([arXiv 2609.24574](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24574)); MindStudio measured average stated confidence of 88% against ~80% actual accuracy, with temperature scaling cutting calibration error by about two-thirds ([MindStudio benchmark](https://www.mindstudio.ai/blog/jev-vs-classic-classifiers-benchmark)); and NavyaAI called raw confidence "severely overconfident" ([NavyaAI](https://www.navyaai.com/blog/jev-typesafe-limitations-production)). Two fragilities are more alarming than miscalibration. A 1,200-decision study found that renaming options from "0/1" to "no/yes" with identical rubrics changed **70.4 answers per hundred** and dropped one model's AUC from .94 to .23, while random-string option names removed the distortion ([arXiv 2609.26758](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26758)); and when the correct option was absent from a Choice, Jev still answered with average confidence 0.79 ([dev.to roundup](https://dev.to/gde/jev-after-eight-days-of-independent-tests-level-with-mid-price-llms-behind-the-frontier-1kln)). Every serious integration therefore does three things: it adds an explicit "none/review" option, it validates the answer is in the allowed set and probabilities sum to about 1.0, and it keeps authorization in application code because "the classifier's judgment cannot give the agent additional access" ([Hugging Face, Eric Kang](https://huggingface.co/blog/karmen-beatapi/how-to-route-an-ai-agent-with-jev-without-forcing); [Vercel use cases](https://vercel.com/i/jev-use-cases)).

Operational maturity is the other constraint. Direct console signups gave a $5 credit but were **paused on 2026-09-22** ([Firecrawl](https://www.firecrawl.dev/blog/what-is-jev)); an unofficial tracker of status.typesafe.ai shows **90-day API availability of 99.827%** with six incidents in the first ten days after launch ([jevaiguide.com](https://jevaiguide.com/is-jev-down/)); rate limits of 1,200 requests per minute and 250,000 tokens per second are "adjusting dynamically" ([TypeSafe Models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models.md)); and LangChain's middleware and Vercel's `experimental_evaluate` are both explicitly experimental ([LangChain](https://www.langchain.com/blog/building-a-harness-with-jev)). Community projects have already added circuit breakers "so an API outage doesn't cost a 12 s timeout on every gated call" ([the-jev-enator #38](https://github.com/jakenbear/the-jev-enator/issues/38)).

## EddiGits should use Jev at three decision points, none of them generation

EddiGits today runs a linear pipeline: Whisper produces timestamped segments, `VectorIndexer.create_chunks` cuts them into **30-second chunks with 5-second overlap**, all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeds them into ChromaDB, and `QueryEngine.search` fetches `top_k * 2` candidates, drops anything below a cosine `MIN_SIMILARITY` of 0.5, keeps ten, and hands the top five to Claude or GPT with a prompt that asks for an `ANSWER:` and a `CONFIDENCE:` line that is then string-parsed. Jev cannot replace any generation step, and the docs are explicit that it is "not trained for text generation; slow and ineffective when forced" ([Model Jaggedness](https://docs.typesafe.ai/model-jaggedness/jev-1.13.md)). It also cannot see audio, so Whisper stays exactly where it is. What Jev can do is make the three judgment calls that currently rest on a raw cosine number and a parsed string.

The first and best-supported fit is **post-retrieval relevance and answerability gating**, which maps directly onto TypeSafe's own RAG passage-classification cookbook: per query/passage pair it asks Noul questions for relevance ("Does this passage address the subject of the query?"), answer evidence ("Does this passage state information usable in a direct answer?"), contradiction and prompt injection, with routing thresholds of relevance <0.45 exclude and evidence >0.55 include; in its 81-passage test an injected forum post that ranked first by embedding similarity scored 0.99 on the injection detector ([TypeSafe RAG cookbook](https://docs.typesafe.ai/cookbooks/classifying_rag_passages.md)). For EddiGits this means replacing the `MIN_SIMILARITY` filter with two calibrated probabilities per chunk and ordering the top five by evidence probability rather than cosine distance. The documented gains for this pattern are modest but consistent: BM25 top-30 re-ranked by a Noul lifted legal top-1 retrieval from 5% to 18% ([TypeSafe rerank cookbook](https://docs.typesafe.ai/cookbooks/rerank_typesafe.md)), and SciFact nDCG@10 went from 66.47 to 75.13 on the same top-20 ([rag-jev](https://pypi.org/project/rag-jev/)). The honest caveat is that EddiGits already runs sentence-transformers locally, and a self-hosted Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B tied Jev on nDCG at about a quarter of the per-query cost ([denser.ai](https://github.com/denser-org/rerank-bench-jev)); the jev-rerank authors call Jev "a cheap first pass, not a cross-encoder" ([CMaintz/jev-rerank](https://github.com/CMaintz/jev-rerank)). Jev's real edge over a local cross-encoder here is not ranking quality but that the same call also returns answerability, injection and any custom criteria, with zero model management.

The second fit is **deciding whether to spend the LLM call at all**. The semantic-find cookbook packs a Choice over line IDs plus a Noul "does the document contain any answer at all" into one request, with 0.7 meaning answered and 0.35 partial, and scales to 255 lines per request ([TypeSafe semantic_find cookbook](https://docs.typesafe.ai/cookbooks/semantic_find.md)). An "answer_exists" Noul over the ten retrieved chunks lets `query()` return "No relevant information found" on a calibrated basis instead of on an embedding threshold, and lets the returned `confidence` field carry a real probability instead of whatever the LLM wrote after `CONFIDENCE:`. On binary yes/no judgments of this kind Jev is "statistically tied with the best of six LLMs at 1/28 of the price" ([Jevals](https://jevals.com/)), and TypeSafe's confidence-routing pattern gives the gating rule: a 0.6 floor for fallback and above 0.85 for autonomous action, where "the worst case outcome determines each threshold" ([TypeSafe confidence routing](https://docs.typesafe.ai/patterns/confidence-routing.md)).

The third fit is **index-time chunk tagging** to support the README's own headline queries ("In which video was I appreciated?", "Show me where I received positive feedback"). Those are not semantic-similarity questions; a chunk about praise does not necessarily embed near the word "appreciated." Running each 30-second chunk through one batched call at index time with questions such as `praise_received` (Noul), `speaker_sentiment` (Score) and `topic` (Choice with an explicit "other" option), and storing the probabilities as SQLite chunk metadata, turns those queries into metadata filters combined with ChromaDB's `where` clause. The closest documented analogue is a sponsor-segment detector that scored one probability per 30-second caption window and caught **77% of SponsorBlock-marked sponsor seconds** across 23 videos at $0.0008 per video ([valentynkit/jev-skip](https://github.com/valentynkit/jev-skip)); comment tagging at 1,000 items for $0.05 shows the cost envelope ([MindStudio](https://www.mindstudio.ai/blog/jev-use-cases-automation)). At $0.042 per million tokens, a one-hour video yields roughly 144 overlapping chunks of a few hundred tokens each including questions, so indexing costs on the order of a fifth of a cent per hour of video (author's estimate from the published price). The caution is that subjective rubrics are Jev's weakest documented area: emotion classification lost "by a wide margin" to a trained encoder and helpfulness scoring did no better than guessing ([MindStudio benchmark](https://www.mindstudio.ai/blog/jev-vs-classic-classifiers-benchmark); [Jevals](https://jevals.com/)). Framing "appreciation" as a concrete Noul ("Does a speaker explicitly thank or praise another person in this passage?") rather than a Score on a vague scale follows the decomposition evidence and Vercel's guidance to use descriptive criteria like "Blocking with no workaround" rather than "high" ([Vercel KB](https://vercel.com/kb/guide/typesafe-jev-and-ai-sdk)).

A fourth, lower-confidence option is **query-intent routing** between the SQLite `search_text` path (exact mentions of "John Smith"), the ChromaDB semantic path, and the tagged-metadata path, using a Choice with an explicit "unsure" option. Routing is Jev's best-evidenced category in general, but the only measured routing benchmarks are complexity tiers for model selection, not retrieval-mode selection, so this should be treated as plausible rather than proven ([LiteLLM](https://docs.litellm.ai/blog/jev-auto-router-benchmark)). Two things Jev should never be asked to do in EddiGits follow directly from the jaggedness page: compare or compute timestamps (dates and times are "read as text, not ordered quantities") and count mentions across chunks ("Jev is not a calculator"); both belong in SQL ([Model Jaggedness](https://docs.typesafe.ai/model-jaggedness/jev-1.13.md)).

## Call it from Python through OpenRouter, with a fallback to today's path

The official SDK is `pip install typesafe-sdk` (Python 3.10+), exposing `TypeSafeClient().system_one(state=..., questions={...})` with `Choice`, `Score` and `Noul` classes, reading `TYPESAFE_API_KEY`, and auto-retrying HTTP 429 and 529 with exponential backoff ([TypeSafe Quick Start](https://docs.typesafe.ai/introduction/quickstart.md); [ekky.dev](https://ekky.dev/blog/typesafe-ai-jev-implementation-on-python/)). Because direct signups are paused, the lowest-friction route today is OpenRouter, which needs only an OpenRouter key, accepts the same contract at `POST https://openrouter.ai/api/v1/systemone` (or its alpha Decisions endpoint), serves `typesafe/jev-1.13` with the alias `~typesafe/jev-latest` at the same $0.042 per million, and returns `usage.cost` in dollars on every response; OpenRouter says the official SDK can be pointed at it with a one-line base-URL change ([OpenRouter docs](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/community/jev)). One measured cost of that route is latency: **734 ms p50 via OpenRouter against 421 ms first-party** ([WallerChen/jev-measured](https://github.com/WallerChen/jev-measured)), and EU-based callers saw 272–809 ms p50 versus roughly 100 ms from the US West Coast ([jev-kit](https://github.com/FlorianRiquelme/jev-kit)). A Python reference implementation of exactly this pattern, a search CLI posting native `state`/`questions` to OpenRouter with `data_collection: deny` and reading `answers[...].noul`, already exists ([larguesa/jev-search](https://github.com/larguesa/jev-search)).

Three engineering rules follow from the evidence. Batch aggressively: "single-question calls waste the architecture," and 30 questions cost the same ~150 ms as one ([Julian Goldie](https://juliangoldie.com/jev-ai-api/); [MindStudio benchmark](https://www.mindstudio.ai/blog/jev-vs-classic-classifiers-benchmark)), so the per-query gate should send the query plus all ten candidate chunks as structured state with one relevance and one evidence Noul per chunk, well inside the 32k budget for 30-second chunks. Pin the model version, because "updates change answers" ([dev.to, Valyu](https://dev.to/valyuai/how-to-use-jev-a-practical-guide-to-typesafes-system-one-model-g5e)). Wrap every call in a short timeout with a deterministic fallback to the existing cosine path, following LiteLLM's production config of a 3,000 ms timeout and circuit breaker ([LiteLLM](https://docs.litellm.ai/blog/jev-auto-router-benchmark)). Before trusting any threshold, hand-label 50 to a few hundred chunks and fit a temperature, which took ECE "from 0.16–0.21 to under 0.025" in one held-out test ([dev.to roundup](https://dev.to/gde/jev-after-eight-days-of-independent-tests-level-with-mid-price-llms-behind-the-frontier-1kln)). Finally, note two untested dimensions that are specific to this application: no published study has run Jev on Whisper or any other ASR output, whose disfluencies and mis-transcriptions are precisely the "unrelated detail" the jaggedness page warns degrades accuracy, and non-English transcripts should expect the documented penalty (Russian XNLI dropped from 88.3% to 77.3%) ([dev.to roundup](https://dev.to/gde/jev-after-eight-days-of-independent-tests-level-with-mid-price-llms-behind-the-frontier-1kln)).

## Conclusion

The interface, not the accuracy, is Jev's innovation: parallel typed questions over shared state with usable probabilities and no parsing is a primitive that mid-tier LLMs can emulate with logprobs and small fine-tuned models can beat on a fixed task, but neither offers it as a zero-setup hosted call for a few thousandths of a cent. Open re-implementations (OpenJev, Laya, Nimble) appeared within a week and a 26B open model on one L4 came within 2.1 points, so the moat is thin and the price is admittedly possibly subsidised; the sensible posture is to adopt the pattern behind a thin interface that can be re-pointed at a local model later.

For EddiGits specifically, the highest-value change is also the smallest: one batched Jev call between ChromaDB and the LLM that returns per-chunk relevance, per-chunk answerability and a single "any answer exists" probability, replacing a cosine threshold and a parsed `CONFIDENCE:` string with calibrated numbers and skipping the expensive generation step when nothing answers. Index-time tagging of praise and sentiment is the second step and the one that most directly serves the README's promised queries, but it is also the step where the evidence is weakest, so it should ship with a labelled evaluation set from the user's own transcripts rather than on faith.
